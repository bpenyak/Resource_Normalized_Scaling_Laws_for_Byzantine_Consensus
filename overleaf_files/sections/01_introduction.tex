\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Permissioned Byzantine Fault Tolerant (BFT) consensus underpins e-governance
systems --- voting, registries, digital identity --- and forces a concrete
procurement question: \emph{how many validators should the network have?}

The count~$\n$ pulls in opposite directions. Throughput argues for small~$\n$
(every validator executes every transaction; PBFT-family protocols exchange
$O(\n^{2})$ messages per block). Safety argues for large~$\n$ (detecting a
coordinated faulty minority concentrates exponentially in~$\n$). The two
regimes have been studied separately; their joint treatment is the subject of
this paper.

Two obstacles intervene. Published power-law fits
$\TPS(\n)\propto\n^{\alpha}$ disagree in magnitude and sometimes in sign ---
our own earlier studies, extrapolated to sixty validators, differ by roughly an
order of magnitude --- because client concurrency is rarely held fixed while
$\n$ varies, so the fitted exponent mixes load-generator scaling with consensus
overhead. And large testbeds are unavailable: packing many validators onto one
host confounds consensus cost with contention for the host CPU.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1)~An \textbf{identifiability theorem} (Section~\ref{sec:model}): if
throughput follows a bi-factor law and concurrency is scaled along a path of
slope~$\lambda$, the single-factor least-squares fit converges to
$\ahat=\gamma\lambda-\beta$; a positive exponent is therefore evidence
about~$\lambda$, not about the protocol
(Theorem~\ref{thm:identifiability}).
(2)~A \textbf{resource-normalized measurement} (\RNM{}) protocol that fixes a
CPU quota~$\q$ per validator independently of~$\n$ and reports
$\TPSn=\TPS/\q$, making the required factorial design feasible on one commodity
host; $\q$-invariance is tested, not assumed.
(3)~Lognormal \textbf{prediction intervals} validated by empirical coverage
rather than by unchecked percentage error.
(4)~Validator-set \textbf{sizing as a chance-constrained program} whose
feasible set is a closed interval $[\nmin,\nmax]$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:window}); an empty interval certifies that no size meets the
requirement.
The detection bound itself is Hoeffding's inequality with a design-effect
correction; we claim nothing new for it beyond supplying the opposing
constraint with measured parameters. Scope is limited to throughput and
detection reliability: cryptography, endpoint security and coercion lie
outside, absolute TPS values are environment-specific, and all measurements
come from one implementation (Hyperledger Besu/QBFT) on one host; cross-client
replication is out of scope (Section~\ref{sec:case}).
