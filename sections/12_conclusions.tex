\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have addressed the problem of choosing the size of a permissioned BFT
validator set for an e-governance workload, in the realistic setting where no
large testbed is available.

Four results support the method.
\emph{First}, the resource-normalized measurement protocol fixes the
per-validator compute budget independently of the validator count, which
separates consensus overhead from host contention and makes the scaling exponent
identifiable from a single commodity machine.
\emph{Second}, the bi-factor scaling law separates client concurrency from
consensus overhead, and the identifiability theorem shows that a single-factor
exponent converges to $\gamma\lambda-\beta$, where $\lambda$ is a property of the
measurement design. A positive single-factor exponent is therefore consistent
with a load generator scaled alongside the network, and is not by itself
evidence that throughput grows with the validator count. This offers a concrete
explanation for the discrepancy between the predictions of our earlier
work~\cite{peniak2026a,peniak2026b} and indicates which of the two designs was
affected.
\emph{Third}, calibrated lognormal prediction intervals, validated by empirical
coverage on held-out validator counts, replace point accuracy figures with a
falsifiable statement.
\emph{Fourth}, coupling the throughput law with a Hoeffding-type detection bound
turns sizing into a chance-constrained program whose feasible set is a closed
interval $[\nmin,\nmax]$, computable in $O(\log\n)$ evaluations. The detection
bound itself is standard; what it contributes is a constraint monotone in the
opposite direction to throughput. An empty interval certifies that no
validator-set size meets the requirement, which is a structural rather than a
provisioning conclusion.

Of the four, we regard the second as the one most likely to outlive the specific
measurements reported here, since it is a statement about estimation rather than
about any particular system.

The entire experimental campaign executes in a public continuous-integration
workflow on one four-core runner, so every result can be reproduced without
dedicated infrastructure.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Protocol-level instrumentation to cover equivocation faults; validation across
several consensus implementations; extension of the feasibility window to
latency and finality constraints in addition to throughput; and online
recalibration of $\beta$ as a deployment grows.
